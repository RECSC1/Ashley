<p>UNC Chapel Hill employee parking permits are priced by salary, running from $350 a year for employees under $40,000 to $2,250 a year for those over $200,000. A University park and ride permit costs $200 flat, and Chapel Hill Transit is fare free. Where you buy a home decides which of those you realistically use.</p>

<p>Ten salary tiers sit between the top and bottom of the zoned range. Permits are prorated weekly and most employees pay by payroll deduction across 24 pay periods, which is precisely why almost nobody notices the annual number.</p>

<p class="article-source">Figures are UNC published rates as of September 2026 and change. Confirm current pricing with UNC Transportation and Parking before you budget around it.</p>

<h2>What UNC parking actually costs</h2>

<p>Here is the part that catches people relocating from somewhere with a free garage.</p>

<h3>Employee permits are priced by salary</h3>

<p>UNC does not charge everyone the same. Zoned employee permits scale with what you earn, across ten tiers, from $350 at the bottom to $2,250 at the top.</p>

<p>Read that again if you are moving here for a physician, faculty, or senior administrative role. The better the offer letter, the higher the parking bill. A household with two UNC affiliated earners in upper tiers can be looking at north of $4,000 a year to park at work.</p>

<p>If you want a guaranteed reserved space, all access with a reserved spot runs $4,780. That is not a typo and it is not most people, but it exists and someone is paying it.</p>

<h3>The park and ride alternative</h3>

<p>A University park and ride permit costs <strong>$200 a year, flat, regardless of salary</strong>.</p>

<p>Same employer. Same campus. Same commute, give or take a bus ride. One tenth the cost at the top of the range.</p>

<p>The Town of Chapel Hill runs its own lots at $21 a month or $2 a day, which works out to about $252 a year if you use it every month.</p>

<h2>Chapel Hill Transit is fare free, and that is the whole trick</h2>

<p>This is the fact that makes the math work, and it is the fact newcomers consistently do not know.</p>

<p><strong>Chapel Hill Transit charges nothing to ride.</strong> Not a discounted fare. Not a subsidized pass through your employer. Zero, on every fixed route, for everyone, because Chapel Hill, Carrboro, and the University split the operating and capital costs between them instead of collecting fares.</p>

<p>This is not a token service either. The system carried 4,305,300 riders in 2025 and runs roughly 18,800 weekday trips.</p>

<p>So the actual choice in front of a UNC employee is not whether to pay for parking or fight for a bus. It is this: pay up to $2,250 to park a car on campus, or pay $200 to park it at a lot and ride in for free.</p>

<p>The reason more people do not take the second option is simple. They bought a house nowhere near a lot.</p>

<h2>What this means for where you buy</h2>

<h3>Neighborhoods with a lot inside them or next door</h3>

<p><strong>Southern Village</strong> has a park and ride lot in the neighborhood. Not nearby. In it. You can walk or make a two minute drive to a lot with free direct service to campus and downtown. For a UNC or UNC Health household, that single amenity is worth more than most of what shows up in a listing description.</p>

<p><strong>Carrboro</strong> sits next to the Jones Ferry lot and is closer to campus than most people expect anyway.</p>

<p><strong>North Chapel Hill</strong>, along the MLK corridor, has both the town Eubanks Road lot and the University MLK Jr. Blvd lot.</p>

<p><strong>Anything off NC 54 on the east side</strong> has the two Friday Center lots, which is a meaningful piece of the case for that side of town.</p>

<p><strong>The Chatham County side</strong>, including <a href="/neighborhoods/briar-chapel-chapel-hill">Briar Chapel</a>, has a University lot on the Pittsboro side. That is a real option and it changes the commute conversation for a part of the market that is usually written off as a pure drive.</p>

<h3>Neighborhoods where you are driving to campus</h3>

<p>Some places put you close enough that the whole question dissolves. <a href="/neighborhoods/meadowmont-chapel-hill">Meadowmont</a> sits about two miles from campus, close enough that a permit, a bike, or a short bus ride are all real answers and none of them dominates.</p>

<p>Others leave you driving and paying. That is not automatically a bad trade. It is just a trade that should be made on purpose, with the number in front of you, instead of discovered in your second August here.</p>

<h2>The ten year number</h2>

<p>Take an employee at the top of the salary range, in a home with no practical park and ride access.</p>

<p>$2,250 a year for a campus permit. Against $200 for a park and ride permit from a neighborhood with a lot. That is <strong>$2,050 a year</strong>, every year, for as long as you hold the job.</p>

<p>Over a ten year hold, roughly <strong>$20,500</strong>.</p>

<p>Two UNC affiliated earners in upper tiers, and you can double it.</p>

<p>Nobody puts that in a listing. No mortgage calculator asks about it. It does not appear in your closing costs, your tax bill, or your HOA dues. It just quietly leaves your paycheck twice a month for a decade.</p>

<p>I am not telling you to pick your house off a parking permit. I am telling you that $20,000 is real money and it belongs in the comparison when you are weighing two neighborhoods against each other.</p>

<h2>What people get wrong about this</h2>

<p><strong>I will just park somewhere and walk.</strong> Campus parking is zoned and enforced, and Chapel Hill downtown parking is not a loophole. This plan lasts about two weeks.</p>

<p><strong>My employer covers it.</strong> Usually not, and when relocation compensation covers a first year, that is one year of a permanent cost.</p>

<p><strong>It is only a few hundred dollars.</strong> Only at the bottom of the salary range. The people most likely to assume this are the people in the tiers where it is not true.</p>

<p><strong>I will bike.</strong> Plenty of people do, and Chapel Hill is reasonably good for it. Then December arrives, or a clinic shift ends at 11pm, and the car comes back. Plan for the car and let the bike be a bonus.</p>

<p><strong>I will figure it out after I move.</strong> You can. It is just much cheaper to figure it out before you pick a neighborhood, because the neighborhood is the part you cannot change later without selling a house.</p>

<h2>If you are relocating for a UNC or UNC Health job</h2>

<p>A few things that are specific to this situation and worth knowing early.</p>

<p><strong>Your start date is probably not negotiable and everyone else has the same one.</strong> Academic and medical appointments cluster on fixed dates. That means a predictable wave of people needing housing in the same window every year, competing for the same inventory. Starting your search when you sign is not early. It is normal.</p>

<p><strong>Test the commute you will actually have.</strong> Not campus generally. Your building, your shift, your hour. UNC Medical Center at 6:30am and a campus office at 9am are two different commutes on two different roads. The <a href="/buyer-tools">commute tool on my site</a> will give you a baseline between any two addresses.</p>

<p><strong>Decide the parking question before the neighborhood question, not after.</strong> It is a ten minute conversation that narrows the map.</p>

<p><strong>Ask about the due diligence fee before you write an offer.</strong> North Carolina contracts include a non-refundable payment made directly to the seller, separate from earnest money. Buyers from other states are consistently caught off guard by it, and you do not want to learn about it under deadline pressure.</p>

<h2>The other costs that move with your address</h2>

<p>Parking is one of several recurring costs in this market that are set by where you buy rather than what you buy.</p>

<p>Property tax is the big one. A home inside Chapel Hill town limits pays the Orange County rate plus the town rate. A home a few minutes away in unincorporated Chatham County, still carrying a Chapel Hill mailing address, pays no municipal tax at all. On an identically priced home that difference runs about $3,000 a year.</p>

<p>Stack the two together and you can find two houses at the same list price with a $5,000 annual gap in what it costs to live in them and get to work. Same price. Same town on the envelope. Very different monthly reality.</p>

<p>This is the part of a Chapel Hill purchase that almost never makes it into the conversation, and it is the part I check on every property before a client gets attached to one.</p>
