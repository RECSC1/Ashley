<p>You typed your address into a website, a number appeared, and now that number is living in your head.</p>
<p>I understand the appeal. It is instant, it is free, and it feels official. But before you build a plan around it, you should know what that number actually is and what it is not.</p>
<p>Zillow publishes its own accuracy data, which I appreciate. For homes that are not currently listed for sale, the Zestimate carries a median error rate of about 7 percent. On a home at the current Chapel Hill median, that is roughly $46,000 in either direction.</p>
<p>Now here is the part most people skip. "Median error" does not mean every estimate is within 7 percent. It means half are closer than that and half are further off. Your home could be the one that is off by twelve percent. There is no way to know from the outside.</p>
<p>Once a home is actually listed, the same tool tightens up considerably, to a median error near 1.9 percent. That is not because the algorithm suddenly got smarter about your house. It is because a real person with a real license put a real price on it and fed the model actual information.</p>
<p>That is the whole story in one sentence. The estimate improves when a human is involved.</p>
<h2>What the Chapel Hill market is actually doing right now</h2>
<p>Before we talk about your house specifically, here is the ground you are standing on.</p>
<div class="stat-callouts"><blockquote><p>Median sale price: $657,642. Up 9.6 percent from a year ago. Chapel Hill has not softened the way a lot of markets have.</p></blockquote>
<blockquote><p>Median days on market: 25. That is eight days slower than last year, which is a real change, but 25 days is still a fast market by any historical standard.</p></blockquote>
<blockquote><p>Sale-to-list ratio: 98.9 percent. Sellers are getting nearly their full asking price. The gap has widened by about two points year over year, which tells you negotiation exists again without telling you sellers are in trouble.</p></blockquote>
<blockquote><p>Homes selling above list: 24.7 percent. Down more than seventeen points from a year ago. One in four homes still draws competition. Three in four do not.</p></blockquote></div>
<p>The average Chapel Hill home receives four offers. This is still a seller's market. It is just a seller's market with conditions attached.</p>
<p class="article-source">(Source: Redfin Chapel Hill market data, June 2026.)</p>
<p>One important note on geography, because it trips people up constantly. Those numbers describe homes inside the Town of Chapel Hill. Widen the map to include the surrounding Chapel Hill addressed properties in Orange, Chatham, and Durham counties and the picture shifts, sometimes significantly, on both price and time on market. A Chapel Hill mailing address is not the same thing as a Chapel Hill town address, and the automated tools frequently blur that line. I wrote about that in more detail in the <a href="/blog/chapel-hill-housing-market-2026">2026 Chapel Hill market breakdown</a>.</p>
<h2>Why automated estimates struggle here specifically</h2>
<p>Algorithms do well in subdivisions where two hundred houses were built from four floor plans in the same eighteen months. Chapel Hill is close to the opposite of that.</p>
<p>County and district lines do not follow addresses. Two homes with the same mailing address can sit in different counties, carry different tax bills, and feed different schools. An estimate that treats them as comparable is comparing two different products.</p>
<p>Our housing stock is genuinely varied. A 1962 ranch near campus, a 2019 build in a planned community, and a custom home on three acres are all "Chapel Hill homes" to a model. They are not remotely the same asset.</p>
<p>Lot quality carries real weight here and is nearly invisible in data. Mature trees, usable flat yard, a creek, a walkout basement, whether your driveway is a gentle slope or a winter event. None of that is in a public record field.</p>
<p>Condition is not in the data at all. The model does not know you replaced the roof, the HVAC, and the windows. It also does not know your kitchen is original to 1988. It assumes average, and average is almost never accurate.</p>
<p>Proximity to UNC and UNC Health is not linear. Walkability to campus behaves differently than a ten minute drive, and the model does not really understand why.</p>
<p>None of this means the online estimate is worthless. It is a fine starting point for curiosity. It is a poor foundation for a six figure decision.</p>

<h2>What actually sets your number</h2>
<p>When I put together a valuation, I am looking at things a model cannot see.</p>
<p>Closed sales within the last 90 days, in your actual district, with genuine similarity. Not "nearby homes." Comparable homes. In a market this varied, three real comps beat thirty loose ones.</p>
<p>Current competition. What a buyer can choose instead of your house, at your price, this week. That set changes constantly and it sets your ceiling more than any historical average.</p>
<p>Condition, honestly assessed. Including the things you have stopped noticing. Buyers with options notice everything.</p>
<p>Which improvements the market pays for. A kitchen refresh and a new roof do not return the same way. Neither does a pool. I would rather tell you not to spend the money than watch you spend it and not get it back.</p>
<p>Timing. Chapel Hill has a rhythm tied to the university calendar and the relocation cycle. Listing three weeks earlier or later can matter more than a five thousand dollar price adjustment.</p>
<p>Your actual goal. Somebody who needs to close by a hard date should price differently than somebody who can wait for the right buyer. Same house, different strategy, different number.</p>
<h2>The mistake that costs Chapel Hill sellers the most</h2>
<p>Overpricing on purpose.</p>
<p>The logic sounds reasonable every time. List high, leave room to negotiate, come down if you have to. In a 25 day market, here is what actually happens.</p>
<p>Your strongest buyer traffic arrives in the first ten to fourteen days. That is when your listing is new, when it hits saved searches, when agents bring people through. If your price is wrong during that window, the buyers who were most ready to act simply look at something else.</p>
<p>Then you reduce. And the buyers who come now are looking at a home with a price cut and a growing days-on-market number, and they read both as leverage. You end up negotiating from a weaker position than if you had priced it correctly on day one.</p>
<p>Correctly priced homes in good condition are still receiving multiple offers in this market. Overpriced homes are still sitting. The market has not stopped rewarding good pricing. It has just stopped forgiving bad pricing.</p>
<h2>What I would do before you list</h2>
<p>Get a real valuation, not an estimate. Actual comps, actual condition, actual competition.</p>
<p>Get a pre-listing inspection. Finding the problem yourself is dramatically cheaper than finding it during due diligence with a buyer holding the leverage.</p>
<p>Handle the cheap things. Paint, lighting, landscaping, decluttering. This is the highest return money in the entire transaction and it is not close.</p>
<p>Confirm your county and school district in writing. Buyers will verify. You should know before they ask.</p>
<p>Decide your timeline before your price. The number depends on the goal.</p>
